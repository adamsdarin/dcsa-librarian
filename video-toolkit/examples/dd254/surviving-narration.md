## Part 1 — "Who holds a copy, and why" (`copies`)
Several people hold a copy of the same DD 254, and each uses it differently. The contractor's FSO turns it into a security program: storage, briefings, training, and guidance for the people doing the work. DCSA uses its copy to oversee the contractor. The form tells DCSA what the company is authorized to access, store, and do. And the government keeps it with the contract file, so anyone who picks up the contract later knows what was agreed.

## Part 2 — `scenarios`
Three quick situations show how items 1a and 1b work together. One: contractor staff work only on a government base, and nothing classified goes to their own office. 1a is Secret, 1b is None, and later, item eleven will say access only elsewhere. Two: the contractor keeps classified reports at its office for reference, but never creates any. 1a is Secret, 1b is Secret, and item eleven will say receive and store documents only. Three: the contractor writes new classified analysis at its own office. 1a and 1b are both Secret, and item eleven will say receive, store, and generate.

## Part 2 — `preaward`
What if bidders need classified information just to write a proposal, say, to review classified specifications? Then they need a facility clearance before they see it, and the GCA can sponsor them during the solicitation itself. If bidders don't need classified access until after award, an uncleared company can still bid, and be sponsored if it wins. Plan the time for that.

*Corrected 4 Oct: the GCA-sponsorship clause, per 32 CFR 117.9(a)(10).*

## Part 3 — `decide11`
If item eleven feels like a puzzle, ask five questions in order. Will their people only see classified information at someone else's site? That's 11a. Will they keep classified documents at their site, but never create any? That's 11b. Will they create classified material? That's 11c. Will they make, modify, or store classified hardware? That's 11d. Alongside 11c, add 11d only if the hardware won't fit in a standard security container. And are they providing a service, with nothing delivered? That's 11e. Then add whatever else applies, from 11f onward.

*Corrected 4 Oct: the 11d sentence, per DCSA instructions 11c(2).*

## Part 4 — `weak13`
Compare two versions of the same entry. Weak: contractor will protect all classified information in accordance with the NISPOM. That tells the contractor nothing it doesn't already know. Strong: reference 11c. Processor performance figures and test results are Secret, per the program's classification guide, provided under separate cover. Unit cost and schedule are unclassified. The strong version names the guide, says what's classified and what isn't, and ties itself to the box it explains.

## Part 4 — `dist`
Item eighteen gives a checklist: the contractor, any subcontractor, the cognizant security office for each, any US activity overseeing security overseas, the administrative contracting officer, and others as needed. Check the boxes that apply, and make sure each one really receives the signed form. If performance is overseas under 11f, the US activity responsible for security there needs a copy too. A form that never reaches DCSA or the contractor's FSO hasn't really been issued.

## Part 5 — `actions`
Then turn the form into a to-do list. 1b means storage: if you're not yet approved at that level, start the request with DCSA now, before material arrives. Each box in item ten means briefings, and sometimes accounts or records. 11c means trained derivative classifiers. 11c or 11d with computers means a system authorization. Item thirteen means guides: confirm every one listed actually arrived. And item fourteen means whatever extra requirements it names, priced into your costs.

## Part 5 — `bidders`
While you're still choosing a subcontractor, the same logic applies. If prospective subcontractors don't need classified access to bid, they don't need a facility clearance to receive your solicitation. If they do need access before award, and aren't cleared, ask DCSA to process them, and allow the time. If a clearance can't be finished in time, DCSA may keep processing it for future work, as long as the delay wasn't the subcontractor's doing.

*Corrected 4 Oct: the final clause, per 32 CFR 117.17(a)(3)(i).*

## Part 6 — "When DCSA visits" (`review`)
Your DD 254s also shape your security reviews. DCSA uses them to see what you're authorized to access, store, and do, and checks that your practices match. So keep every current DD 254 on file, with its revisions, and the guides it lists. If your storage, your computers, or your people don't line up with what the forms authorize, expect questions.
