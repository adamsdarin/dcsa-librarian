# Your Security Clearance, Step by Step: final narration

**Runtime:** 5:50. **Voice:** Kokoro "emma" (British female) at 0.95x, generated locally in the container.
**Script:** your revision, with the corrections listed below. Audience: new applicants.

| Time | Scene | Narration (as captioned) |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | Title | Here's how a security clearance works, in about five minutes. |
| 0:05 | Meet Alex | Meet Alex. Alex just accepted a job that needs a security clearance. A clearance is the government's decision that you're eligible for access to classified information. For contractor employees, that decision belongs to DCSA: the Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency. |
| 0:25 | Before you start | First, the basics. Only an organization with a facility clearance, or a federal agency, can sponsor you for security clearance eligibility. And access to classified information is only granted on a need-to-know basis. There are no "just in case" clearances. If you need access on day one, the process can start pre-employment. But if the employment is with a private company, that company is required to have you in a cleared position within 45 days of your eligibility being granted. This video follows a contractor employee like Alex. If you'll work directly for a federal agency, its security office will guide you, and some later steps differ. |
| 1:06 | Step 1: Your FSO starts the request | Step one. You will need to show proof of U.S. citizenship to your Facility Security Officer, or FSO. Then your FSO will start your investigation request, and you get an email invitation to complete the eApp in NBIS, DCSA's online application. |
| 1:24 | Step 2: You complete the SF 86 in eApp | Step two is yours. In eApp, you complete the SF 86, the Questionnaire for National Security Positions. It asks where you've lived, worked, and studied, plus foreign contacts, travel, finances, and more. You should gather records first, so you're prepared for the questionnaire. Be complete and be honest. Leaving something out can hurt you more than the thing itself. You'll also sign release forms and get your fingerprints taken electronically. Heads up: a newer form, the Personnel Vetting Questionnaire, is being phased in to replace the SF 86. |
| 1:59 | Step 3: Your FSO reviews it | Step three. Your FSO reviews the form for completeness. What you disclose isn't shared around the company. The NISPOM strictly prohibits that. Once the FSO review is complete, it will be released to DCSA, along with your fingerprints. |
| 2:15 | Step 4: DCSA checks the package | Step four. DCSA checks that everything is there. If something's missing, it goes back to your FSO, and you fix it and resubmit. |
| 2:25 | Step 5: Temporary eligibility | Step five. While the full investigation runs, DCSA may grant temporary eligibility. You may hear it called an "interim." It's based on your form and early checks, and it can let you start cleared work sooner. It has limits, and not getting temporary eligibility is not a denial. It just means you wait for the final decision. |
| 2:46 | Step 6: The background investigation | Step six: the background investigation. DCSA checks records like criminal history and credit. Investigators may interview you, your references, and people who know you. Top Secret investigations dig deeper. If you speak to an investigator, answer promptly and honestly. And if you're unsure an investigator is legitimate, DCSA can verify them. You can contact DCSA by calling 878-274-1186. |
| 3:16 | Step 7: Adjudication | Step seven: adjudication. A DCSA adjudicator weighs your case against 13 national adjudicative guidelines, set out in Security Executive Agent Directive 4. They look at many areas, including allegiance, foreign influence, finances, drug use, and personal conduct. You are looked at as a whole person. A single bad moment in time doesn't automatically mean a denial. Time, honesty, and changed behavior can mitigate past issues. Most cases end here, with a favorable decision. Some are approved with conditions and extra monitoring. |
| 3:52 | Step 8: Issues? DOHA reviews | If the adjudicator can't decide in your favor, the case goes to DOHA, the Defense Office of Hearings and Appeals. If DOHA's attorneys agree there's a real concern, you receive a Statement of Reasons, or SOR, listing each one. If they don't agree, eligibility can still be granted. |
| 4:11 | Step 9: You respond to the SOR | Step nine: if you receive an SOR and want to pursue the clearance, you will need to respond. You have 20 days to answer in writing, under oath, admitting or denying each allegation. You can choose: a hearing before an administrative judge, or a decision on the written record. You may bring a lawyer or a personal representative. If you don't respond at all, the clearance is denied. |
| 4:35 | Step 10: Decision and appeal | Step ten: the judge decides. Either side can appeal to the DOHA Appeal Board within 15 days. A final denial means waiting one year before your employer can apply again. |
| 4:48 | Access | Now, let's say Alex is approved. Being eligible isn't the same as access. Before seeing anything classified, Alex gets an initial security briefing and signs the SF 312, a nondisclosure agreement that lasts for life. And Alex only sees what the job requires. That's called need-to-know. |
| 5:08 | Continuous vetting | A clearance isn't one and done. Cleared people are enrolled in continuous vetting, with automated record checks and an updated form about every five years. Your part: report life changes to your FSO, like foreign travel and contacts, money trouble, or an arrest. |
| 5:26 | Recap | So: your FSO starts it. You complete eApp. DCSA reviews, investigates, and decides, with DOHA stepping in if there are issues. Then you're briefed, you sign, and you keep reporting. Tell the truth, meet your deadlines, and talk to your FSO. |
| 5:43 | End card | *(no narration: sources, the investigator-verification number, and the disclaimer)* |
